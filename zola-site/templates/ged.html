GED vs High School Diploma

The GED is marketed as equivalent. The data says otherwise.

The General Educational Development (GED) test is widely sold as a "second chance" for people who didn't finish high school. But the research is clear: GED holders are statistically indistinguishable from dropouts on many key measures — and far below diploma holders. Here's the proof.

How We Got Here

The GED was created in 1942–1943 at the request of the U.S. Armed Forces Institute. Thousands of young men had left high school early to serve in World War II — the military needed a way for them to prove they had the equivalent of a diploma so they could access the GI Bill when they got home. The test was originally called the Tests of General Educational Development, and it was designed on purpose to be passable by someone who learned on the job rather than in a classroom.

That origin story matters. The GED was never meant to be an educational standard — it was a convenience measure for a specific wartime problem. It was designed to let veterans demonstrate ability, not to certify they'd completed a curriculum. When the Servicemen's Readjustment Act of 1944 (the GI Bill) made GED holders eligible for college funding and home loans, the credential's purpose was cemented: not education, but access.

Over the decades the GED expanded far beyond veterans. By the 1960s it was available to any adult dropout. States adopted it as a cheaper alternative to adult education programs. Today roughly 500,000 people take the GED each year. But the test's DNA hasn't changed — it was built as a shortcut, and the labor market treats it like one.

⚠️

They Had to Name It "Equivalent" — Because It Isn't

The word "equivalent" isn't a description — it's a marketing decision. The American Council on Education coined "Tests of General Educational Development" and later marketed the credential as "high school equivalency" specifically to block states from issuing actual wartime diplomas to veterans. States wanted to just give veterans diplomas. ACE lobbied against that — they needed the GED to succeed to validate their ideology that testing could replace classroom learning.

Many GED certificates still have "equivalency diploma" printed on them. The word is designed to make employers, colleges, and holders themselves believe the credential means the same thing as a diploma. It doesn't. The data on this page proves it. The name is the scam.

ACE lobbying against wartime diplomas: Lois M. Quinn, "An Institutional History of the GED" (2003). ACE president George Zook (1946): "any measures which schools... apply to veterans will commend themselves immediately... as policies which they may also follow with respect to nonveterans."

The System Gets Its Money's Worth

Social Security's full retirement age is now 66–67. A GED/dropout holder who dies at 75 collects maybe 8–9 years of benefits after paying into the system for 40+ years. A college-educated person who lives to 87 collects for 20+ years. The math works out real nice for the government — extract decades of physical labor, pay out a fraction of the benefits.

And it's worse than that because the jobs available without a diploma are the ones that destroy your body — construction, warehouse, factory, agriculture. So they're broken down AND dying sooner. The 12-year life expectancy gap isn't just abstract "years of life" — it's years where your knees still work, where you can actually do something with retirement.

What the Government Actually Saves Per GED Holder

Let's run the numbers for a typical GED holder earning ~$44,720/year over a 40-year career:

Social Security: They pay ~$3,421/year in employee FISA taxes + their employer pays ~$3,421/year = $6,842/year into the system. Over 40 years, that's $273,680 contributed. But if they die at 75 instead of 85, they collect roughly $181,440 in benefits (8 years × $1,890/month). The government pockets the difference: ~$92,000 per person.

Medicare: They pay ~$1,033/year in Medicare taxes for 40 years = $41,320 contributed. If they die at 75, they collect ~10 years of Medicare vs 20 for a college grad. The government saves roughly $85,000 per person in avoided Medicare costs (CMS data: ~$8,500/year average Medicare spending per beneficiary).

The "Too Rich to Qualify, Too Poor to Survive" Trap: At $44,720/year, a GED holder earns too much for Medicaid (limit ~$20,000 for a single adult in most states) but too little to afford private insurance ($500–$800/month). They're stuck in the gap — working full-time, paying taxes, contributing to systems that won't catch them when they need it. They pay into Social Security and Medicare their whole lives, then die before collecting their fair share.

Total government savings per early-death GED holder: ~$177,000. Multiply that by the roughly 40 million Americans with a GED as their highest credential, and the system saves trillions over the lifetime of that generation.

FISA tax rate: 6.2% (SS) + 1.45% (Medicare) = 7.65% employee + 7.65% employer. Average SS benefit: $1,890/month (SSA 2024). Medicare spending: ~$8,500/beneficiary/year (CMS 2024). Medicaid eligibility: varies by state, typically ~200% FPL. GED holder population: ~40 million (NCES).

The Arrest-to-Prison Pipeline: GED Holders Can't Beat the Charges

Here's what the chart doesn't show you: GED holders get arrested more AND convicted more. Look at the math: 69% of GED holders get arrested, 31% end up in prison — that's a 45% arrest-to-prison conversion rate. Diploma holders? 35% arrested, 8% imprisoned — only 23% conversion. GED holders are nearly twice as likely to be convicted when charged with the same exact crime.

Why? They can't afford lawyers. They take plea bargains because the alternative is worse. They sit in pretrial detention (which increases guilty pleas by 46%). And when they do get sentenced, they're 10% less likely to get probation than diploma holders. The system doesn't just arrest more GED holders — it convicts more of them per arrest (U.S. Sentencing Commission 2023, Franklin 2013, Vera Institute 2020).

The Smoking Outlier

GED holders have the highest smoking prevalence of any education level — higher than dropouts who never finished high school. This breaks the otherwise consistent inverse relationship between education and smoking.

Who Profits From the Test

The GED isn't just an educational policy — it's a business. And the people who run it have made that very clear.

In 2011, the American Council on Education (ACE) partnered with Pearson — the world's largest for-profit education company — to form GED Testing Service. The test went from a nonprofit credential to a for-profit product. The price doubled overnight from ~$60 to $120.

ACE's gross revenue from the GED exceeded $17 million per year before the Pearson deal. Pearson's North American education division alone brings in nearly $1 billion in profit. The GED is a product, and it's sold like one — with "flash sales," study bundles, and social media marketing aimed at the most vulnerable test-takers.

The DOJ Bureau of Prisons has an $861,000 contract with GED Testing Service for prison testing. 10% of all GED tests in the US are administered in prisons. The GED has become the de facto prison diploma — over 60% of formerly incarcerated people with a high school credential hold a GED, not a diploma.

The House Always Wins

You know how lottery players think "if I just play enough times, I'll win"? The GED works the same way. People think "if I just get the GED, things will turn around." But the odds are stacked like a casino:

GED holder getting a college degree: 3.7% — worse than the odds of a roulette bet on a single number (2.7%). The casino gives you better odds than the GED gives you of finishing college.

Bottom quintile → top quintile: 7.5% — the same odds as flipping a coin three times and getting heads every time. You'd need to be born 13 times in the right family to guarantee ending up on top.

GED holder going to prison: 31% — nearly 1 in 3. That's better odds than your GED leading to a degree. The test that's "equivalent" to a diploma is more likely to lead you to a cell than to a classroom.

And here's the part nobody tells you: even when it works, it barely works. Young people from poverty who had strong academic outcomes in high school — who actually beat the odds — ended up earning no more at age 30 than the average student who was simply born into a more affluent family. The floor is higher, but the ceiling is the same. You're running harder just to stay in place.

Roulette odds:2.7% (European). Coin flip: 12.5% for 3 heads. Lottery: Powerball 1 in292M. Chetty et al. (2014): intergenerational mobility data. TNTP "Paths of Opportunity" (2024): poverty + strong academics = still only matches average affluent income.

The real math isn't complicated. Finish high school. That's it. A diploma holder is 12x more likely to get a college degree, 4x less likely to go to prison, 18 percentage points more likely to work full-time, and earns $9,900 more per year — every year, for 40 years. The GED isn't a second chance. It's a consolation prize that costs you $396,000.
